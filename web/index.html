01 · The case

One process, five Earth-system components

Indonesia holds one of the largest tropical peat carbon stocks on Earth. When it's wet, peat doesn't burn. When it dries out, it can smoulder for weeks. Every link in that chain can be measured from orbit, so we treat it as a detective case: what is changing, where, by how much, and is it significant?

Why it matters

Peat fires are among the largest single carbon pulses on the planet in El Niño years, and the haze reaches tens of millions of people in Indonesia, Malaysia and Singapore.

Experiment site

Sumatra and Borneo (95–119.5° E, 6.5° S–7.5° N; islands traced from the MODIS land/water mask), with climate sampled at 10 peat-dome hotspots in Riau, Jambi, South Sumatra and West, Central and South Kalimantan.

What we compare

Before vs after the 2016 regime shift, El Niño vs neutral years, and Sumatra vs Borneo.

    03 · Research, data, analysis & methods

    How we know what we claim

    Research question

    Has a decade of peat-fire governance broken the ocean → drought → fire → haze chain over Sumatra and Borneo? Is the decline significant, and is the record 2026 El Niño putting it to the test?

    Hypotheses

    1. H1 (trend): fire-season fire pixels show a significant monotonic trend over 2003–2025.
    2. H2 (mechanism): year-to-year variability is explained by a measurable chain: ENSO/IOD → rainfall → root-zone soil wetness → fire → aerosol.
    3. H3 (regime): after removing the climate signal, fire shows a structural break consistent with the post-2015 policy response (peat restoration agency, moratoria, stronger enforcement).
    4. H4 (geography): the trend is not uniform. Some regions decline while others hold or rise ("same driver, opposite signs").

    Modeling approach

    • Domain: Sumatra + Borneo (land from the MODIS MOD44W mask), 95–119.5° E, 6.5° S–7.5° N, 0.25° grid for fire and 0.5° grid for trend tests.
    • Period: 2000–2025 observed (2003–2025 for statistics, full Terra+Aqua years) and 2026 as far as the data reach.
    • Unit of analysis: the Jul–Oct fire season (about —% of all fire pixels in the domain).
    • Model: a chain of four cross-validated regressions plus a structural-break term, used to explain past seasons, not to forecast.
    • Comparisons: before vs after 2016, El Niño vs neutral years, Sumatra vs Borneo.

    Expected outcomes & key figures

    1. Trend + significance board, plus the FDR-tested spatial trend map (what, where, how much).
    2. Climate-expected vs observed fire (the regime gap) with the Pettitt change point.
    3. A 2026 check: how this year's measured dryness compares with the record.

    1 · Trend detection

    Mann–Kendall statistic S = Σi<j sgn(xj − xi), with tie-corrected variance and the Hamed–Rao (1998) effective-sample-size correction for autocorrelated series. The magnitude is Sen's slope β = median((xj − xi)/(j − i)), reported per year and as % of the mean per decade. As a sensor check, the trend is repeated on Aqua-only and Terra-only counts.

    2 · Where: field significance

    Each 0.5° cell with ≥ 50 fire pixels gets its own Mann–Kendall test. Hundreds of tests produce false positives by chance, so p-values are controlled with the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery rate (Wilks 2016), α = 0.05.

    3 · Change point

    Pettitt (1979) test, on raw fire pixels and on residuals of ln(fire) regressed on soil wetness. Testing the residual asks whether fire dropped beyond what weather explains.

    4 · Lagged links

    Monthly values are de-seasonalised (each month minus its 2003–2025 mean). We then compute Pearson correlations at 0–6 month lags along the chain.

    Key results

      The fitted chain (2003–2025, n = 23)

      LOO R² = leave-one-year-out cross-validated skill. Fire is modelled in logs because it responds multiplicatively to drying. The regime term is a 0/1 indicator from the Pettitt break year.

      How the chain fits together

      Each link is a regression on the one before it: rain = a + b·ONI + c·DMI → soil = d + e·rain → ln F = f + g·soil + h·regime → AOD = i + j·ln F. The regime coefficient h measures how many fewer fires the same soil wetness produced after 2016.

      Limitations

      • Fire pixels ≠ burned area. MODIS flags 1 km pixels with active fire at overpass time; an 8-day composite counts each burning pixel once. It misses fires under thick smoke or cloud, and smouldering peat with low radiative power. Treat counts as an index.
      • Orbit drift. Terra and Aqua have drifted from their nominal overpass times since about 2020–2022. The Aqua-only trend (—) is shown next to the combined one; part of any late decline could be observational.
      • Reanalysis climate. MERRA-2 rainfall and soil wetness are model-assimilated fields at 0.5°, sampled at 10 points, not in-situ peat water tables.
      • No peat map. None of the listed sources provides peat extent, so the analysis cannot separate peat fires from mineral-soil fires. Borneo includes its Malaysian and Bruneian parts, because no listed source provides national borders.
      • Small n. 23 seasons (2003–2025). Coefficients are robust in cross-validation, but extreme inputs (e.g. ONI > 2) are near or beyond the fitted range.
      • Regime = attribution by elimination. The post-2016 drop is what remains after climate. It is consistent with policy, but could also include land-use change (already-burned or converted peat).

      What would make it stronger

      • MODIS MCD64A1 burned area (in AppEEARS) for hectares burned.
      • SMAP soil moisture (in AppEEARS) for a second, independent dryness signal.
      • A satellite aerosol product in Giovanni (e.g. MODIS AOD) for a smoke check independent of MERRA-2.
      • VIIRS fire products (in AppEEARS, 2012+) for small-fire sensitivity.
      • Station rainfall from NOAA Climate Data Online to check MERRA-2 rain on the ground.
